# 0981.HK — SMIC (Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corp) — Buffett Framework Analysis

## 1. Business Overview
SMIC (0981.HK) is China's largest semiconductor foundry, manufacturing integrated circuits at 14nm to 28nm process nodes for domestic Chinese fabless chip designers and certain international clients. The company is strategically critical to China's national technology self-sufficiency agenda and operates with significant government support (subsidies, preferential tax treatment, state-affiliated shareholder ownership). SMIC is structurally constrained from advancing to leading-edge nodes (sub-10nm) due to US-led export controls restricting access to advanced lithography equipment (notably ASML's EUV machines and US-origin deposition/etch tools). The company is listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange and Shanghai STAR Market.

## 2. Current Metrics (approx. as of May 2026)
- P/E ratio: ~15–25x (highly volatile; wafer demand cycles create sharp earnings swings)
- P/B ratio: ~1.0–1.5x (relatively modest given state subsidies supporting book value)
- ROE: Low and variable (~5–12%); high capex intensity suppresses returns
- Revenue growth (3yr CAGR): Uncertain/negative to low single digit; US sanctions created demand disruption and technology uncertainty
- Debt/Equity: Moderate (~0.5–1.0x); largely funded by state banks and government equity injections
- Dividend yield: Very low (~0.5–1%); capital is directed toward massive wafer capacity expansion

## 3. Moat Assessment
**Moat type identified:** None (at global competitive level); partial low-cost/scale moat within China's domestic captive market
**Closest Berkshire analog:** No analog — Buffett has never held a government-directed foundry or a semiconductor manufacturer
**Buffett quote applied:** "I don't know what a business is worth if I can't predict what it will earn in 10 years. Technology companies that are changing rapidly are outside my circle of competence." — Warren Buffett, Berkshire Annual Meeting, 1998
**Moat verdict:** WEAK
**Reasoning:** SMIC's moat assessment must distinguish between two different competitive arenas. Within China's domestic semiconductor market — where geopolitical forces are actively redirecting demand to domestic suppliers — SMIC has meaningful scale advantages and de facto captive customers. However, in the global foundry industry, SMIC is structurally disadvantaged versus TSMC (2nm–3nm), Samsung (3nm), and GlobalFoundries: it cannot access leading-edge process technology due to equipment restrictions, and its trailing-node (28nm+) products face commoditisation pressure. A moat dependent on government policy and trade barriers rather than intrinsic business superiority is fragile by Buffett's definition — it can evaporate with a policy change. Pricing power at trailing nodes is minimal.

## 4. Management Assessment
**Capital allocation grade:** C
**Owner-mindset signals:**
- Zhao Haijun and Liang Mong Song bring genuine semiconductor industry expertise (former TSMC engineers) — technically capable leadership
- The strategic decision to invest aggressively in 28nm capacity (where SMIC can still compete) reflects pragmatic adaptation to equipment restrictions
- Long-term orientation is visible in multi-year capex commitments building domestic capacity
**Red flags:**
- Capital allocation is substantially government-directed rather than shareholder-directed — the primary objective is national technology self-sufficiency, not return maximisation
- Management operates under constant external constraints (sanctions, equipment availability) that limit strategic optionality in ways no private-sector management team faces
- Related-party dynamics with state-owned shareholders create governance opacity
- The company cannot make independent decisions about which technologies to pursue — a fundamental departure from owner-operated management Buffett requires
**Management verdict:** FAIL
**Reasoning:** Buffett's management standard requires able and trustworthy managers acting in shareholders' interests as the primary mandate. SMIC's management team, however technically competent, operates within a framework where national strategic objectives frequently supersede shareholder returns. Buffett's "institutional imperative" concept applies in an extreme form here: the institution (Chinese state) dictates strategy in ways that are not transparent, predictable, or shareholder-aligned. This is disqualifying under Buffett's framework regardless of individual manager quality.

## 5. Price Assessment
**Current P/E vs Buffett benchmark (≤20x preferred, ≤15x ideal):** At 15–25x, the P/E range spans both acceptable and concerning territory — but given weak moat and earnings volatility, even 15x is not cheap
**Intrinsic value comment:** Rich to very rich given the structural constraints on technology advancement; earnings 10 years out are genuinely unknowable given geopolitical trajectory uncertainty; deep value (P/B ~1x) might attract a Graham-style investor but not Buffett
**Price verdict:** FAIL
**Reasoning:** Price assessment cannot be separated from predictability — Buffett refuses to value businesses whose 10-year earnings he cannot estimate with reasonable confidence. SMIC's earnings in 2036 depend on: US-China geopolitical resolution, Chinese domestic semiconductor demand, whether SMIC can develop leading-edge capability independently (extremely capital-intensive and uncertain), and global wafer pricing cycles. This is not a business Buffett can value, therefore any price is potentially wrong. The geopolitical "too hard" pile is appropriate here.

## 6. Circle of Competence Note
SMIC is emphatically outside Buffett's circle of competence on multiple independent dimensions: semiconductor manufacturing process technology (which Buffett has explicitly excluded), geopolitical risk exposure (US-China technology war), government-directed capital allocation, and Hong Kong/China market governance norms. Buffett placed virtually all semiconductor and technology hardware businesses in the "too hard" pile until Apple in 2016 — and Apple qualified as a consumer products company, which SMIC manifestly is not.
